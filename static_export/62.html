India’s Rural Health Care System — Primary Centres and Policy Gaps

The Hindu | Date: 2024 • ~600 words

Introduction

India’s health outcomes are deeply influenced by the quality of its rural healthcare system, which serves nearly 65% of the population. In 2024, The Hindu highlighted persistent shortages of staff, infrastructure, and medicines in Primary Health Centres (PHCs) and Sub-Centres. Introduction

Current Status
• Sub-Centres: ~1.5 lakh operational (target: 1 per 5,000 population).
• PHCs: ~25,000 (target: 1 per 30,000 population).
• CHCs (Community Health Centres): ~5,000 (1 per 1.2 lakh population).
• Human Resources: ~25% doctor shortage, ~40% nurse/ANM shortage (RHS 2023).
• Funding: Public health expenditure ~2.1% of GDP (target: 2.5% by 2025).
________________________________________
Key Challenges
1. Staff Shortages: Doctors unwilling to serve in rural/remote areas.
2. Infrastructure Deficit: Many PHCs lack labs, electricity, internet.
3. Medicine Availability: Frequent stock-outs; weak supply chains.
4. Referral Weakness: Poor linkages from PHCs to CHCs and district hospitals.
5. Social Barriers: Rural women and Dalit/Adivasi communities face access discrimination.
________________________________________
Government Measures
• National Health Mission (NHM): Funding support to strengthen PHCs, ASHA workers.
• Ayushman Bharat (2018):
o Health & Wellness Centres (HWCs) upgrade PHCs.
o PM-JAY for secondary/tertiary insurance coverage.
• Digital Health Mission: Telemedicine and e-health records.
• Incentives for Doctors: Rural postings tied to PG admissions.
• Jan Aushadhi Kendras: Affordable generic medicines.
________________________________________
Achievements
• HWCs: >1.6 lakh centres functional by 2024.
• Telemedicine Uptake: e-Sanjeevani platform crossed 14 crore consultations.
• ASHA Recognition: Awarded WHO’s Global Health Leaders’ Award (2022).
________________________________________
Persistent Gaps
• Underfunding: Per capita health spend far below global average.
• Uneven Access: Some states (Kerala, Tamil Nadu) excel, others lag (Bihar, UP).
• Technology Divide: Rural poor often excluded from digital health tools.
• Overburdened ASHAs: Inadequate pay, high workload.
________________________________________
Significance
• Public Health: Strong rural healthcare reduces disease burden.
• Social Justice: Health equity across caste, gender, geography.
• Economic Productivity: Healthy rural workers improve agriculture/industry.
• Democracy: Effective healthcare builds citizen trust in governance.
________________________________________
UPSC Relevance
• GS-II (Governance & Welfare): Health policy, rural institutions.
• GS-III (Economy & Social Justice): Human development.
• Essay:
o “Health is not a privilege but a right.”
o “India’s rural future depends on rural healthcare.”
• PSIR Paper II: Welfare state and social contract.
________________________________________
Way Forward
1. Increase Public Spending: Move toward 2.5% GDP target.
2. Strengthen HWCs: Upgrade infrastructure, labs, digital tools.
3. Incentivise Rural Doctors: Better pay, housing, career pathways.
4. Empower ASHAs: Regularise pay, training, and recognition.
5. Community Participation: Panchayats and SHGs in health monitoring.
________________________________________
Conclusion

Conclusion

India’s rural healthcare system remains overstretched yet indispensable. Strengthening PHCs and HWCs with adequate staff and funding is vital for universal health coverage. For UPSC aspirants, it highlights how social justice, governance, and human development intersect in policy.

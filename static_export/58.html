India’s Renewable Energy Push — Opportunities and Bottlenecks

The Hindu | Date: 2024 • ~600 words

Introduction

India has emerged as a global leader in renewable energy, ranked 4th in wind power and 5th in solar capacity. In 2024, The Hindu highlighted both the achievements and bottlenecks in scaling renewables toward India’s 500 GW non-fossil target by 2030. Introduction

Current Status
• Installed Renewable Capacity (2024): ~180 GW (solar 70 GW, wind 44 GW, hydro 50 GW, biomass 10 GW).
• Target: 500 GW by 2030.
• Global Standing: Among top 5 countries for renewables investment.
• Green Hydrogen: Mission launched in 2023 with ₹19,744 crore outlay.
________________________________________
Achievements
1. Cost Competitiveness: Solar/wind cheaper than coal in many regions.
2. Foreign Investment: $15 billion inflows since 2020.
3. Policy Support: RPOs (Renewable Purchase Obligations), Viability Gap Funding.
4. International Cooperation: ISA (International Solar Alliance) with >100 members.
5. Employment: Renewable sector employs ~1.5 million workers.
________________________________________
Challenges
1. Grid Integration: Renewable intermittency creates supply instability.
2. Land Conflicts: Solar parks face displacement, ecological concerns.
3. Financial Stress: DISCOMs’ poor health limits renewable uptake.
4. Technology Dependence: Reliance on imported solar PV cells and battery storage.
5. Slow Offshore Wind: India yet to exploit its vast coastal potential.
________________________________________
Government Initiatives
• National Solar Mission (2010): Expanded to 100 GW solar target.
• Wind-Solar Hybrid Policy (2018): Integration of renewable sources.
• Green Bonds: Financing renewable infrastructure.
• PLI Scheme: ₹24,000 crore for solar module manufacturing.
• Battery Storage Policy: Draft under consultation.
________________________________________
Global Dimension
• COP28: India reaffirmed net-zero by 2070.
• Energy Diplomacy: Partnerships with EU, US, and Japan for hydrogen and renewables.
• South-South Leadership: ISA demonstrates India’s soft power.
________________________________________
Significance
• Climate Goals: Renewable expansion central to emission cuts.
• Energy Security: Reduces dependence on imported coal/oil.
• Economic Growth: Green jobs and investment opportunities.
• Technological Leapfrog: Potential to become hub for hydrogen and storage.
________________________________________
UPSC Relevance
• GS-III (Economy & Environment): Energy transition, climate policy.
• GS-II (IR): Climate diplomacy.
• Essay:
o “Renewables are not an option but an imperative.”
o “Energy transition is the bridge between sustainability and growth.”
• PSIR Paper II: Global environmental governance.

Way Forward
1. Grid Modernisation: Smart grids, flexible storage systems.
2. Domestic Manufacturing: Support solar and battery industries.
3. Finance Mobilisation: Green bonds, sovereign guarantees, blended finance.
4. Balanced Land Use: Rooftop solar, agro-photovoltaics, offshore wind.
5. Regional Cooperation: Cross-border renewable energy trade in South Asia.
________________________________________
Conclusion

Conclusion

India’s renewable journey is a success story in progress, but bottlenecks in finance, technology, and governance must be overcome. For UPSC aspirants, it highlights how climate policy, economy, and diplomacy converge in India’s development trajectory.

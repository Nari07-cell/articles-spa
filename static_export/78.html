India’s Air Pollution Crisis — Causes, Impacts, and Policy Measures

Indian Express | Date: 2024 • ~600 words

Introduction

Air pollution remains one of India’s most pressing environmental and public health challenges. In 2024, Indian Express reported that Delhi, Lucknow, and several industrial clusters continued to record AQI levels in the “severe” category, despite policy interventions. Introduction

Current Status
• Deaths Attributable (Lancet, 2023): ~2 million annually in India.
• Economic Cost: ~1.4% of GDP lost due to productivity decline and health expenses.
• Major Pollutants: PM2.5, PM10, NOx, SO2, ozone.
• Hotspots: Delhi NCR, Indo-Gangetic Plain, industrial clusters.
• Global Rank: India among worst in AQI rankings.
________________________________________
Causes
1. Vehicular Emissions: Growing urban traffic, diesel use.
2. Industrial Pollution: Thermal power plants, brick kilns, steel and cement.
3. Agricultural Burning: Paddy stubble burning in Punjab–Haryana.
4. Construction Dust: Rapid urbanisation without dust control.
5. Household Sources: Biomass burning in rural homes.
________________________________________
Government Measures
• National Clean Air Programme (NCAP, 2019): Target 40% reduction in PM2.5 levels by 2026.
• Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM, 2020): Oversight in NCR.
• BS-VI Emission Norms (2020): Cleaner fuel and engines.
• Ujjwala Yojana: 9 crore LPG connections to reduce indoor air pollution.
• Renewables Push: Reduce dependence on coal power.
________________________________________
Achievements
• NCAP Coverage: 131 cities preparing action plans.
• Electric Mobility: EV adoption accelerated under FAME-II.
• Cleaner Cooking: LPG coverage >95%.
• Awareness: Media and civil society engagement increasing.
________________________________________
Persistent Gaps
• Weak Enforcement: Industries and vehicles bypass norms.
• Crop Burning Continuation: Farmers lack viable alternatives.
• Urban Governance Issues: Poor dust control, weak monitoring.
• Health System Strain: Few hospitals track pollution-linked diseases.
________________________________________
Significance
• Public Health: Air pollution causes respiratory, cardiac, and developmental disorders.
• Economy: Worker productivity and tourism suffer.
• Environment: Contributes to climate change (black carbon, methane).
• Social Justice: Poor and children most affected.
________________________________________

• GS-III (Environment): Pollution, mitigation.

Way Forward
1. Enforce Crop Alternatives: Subsidies for happy seeders, biomass markets.
2. Strengthen NCAP: City-level accountability and funding.
3. Mass EV Adoption: Incentives for public and freight transport.
4. Industrial Compliance: Strict monitoring and penalties.
5. Health Interventions: Air clinics, real-time AQI alerts, citizen protection measures.
________________________________________
Conclusion

Conclusion

Air pollution in India is both an environmental and social justice crisis. Stronger enforcement, technological alternatives, and people’s participation are critical to ensuring cleaner air. For UPSC aspirants, this issue illustrates how health, governance, and environment intersect in policymaking.

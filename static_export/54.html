India’s Climate Finance Challenge — Mobilising Resources for Green Growth

The Hindu | Date: 2024 • ~600 words

Introduction

India has committed to ambitious climate goals — 500 GW of non-fossil fuel capacity by 2030 and net-zero emissions by 2070. Yet, as The Hindu highlighted in 2024, the real bottleneck is financing the transition. Estimates suggest India needs over $10 trillion by 2070 to decarbonise, but domestic and international flows remain inadequate. Climate finance thus becomes a key test for India’s economic policy, diplomacy, and sustainability agenda. ________________________________________

Introduction

Current Status of Climate Finance
• Domestic Budget Allocation (2024–25): ₹35,000 crore for energy transition and green hydrogen.
• Green Bonds: India issued sovereign green bonds worth ₹16,000 crore.
• FDI & Multilateral Support: Some flows from World Bank, ADB, and JICA.
• Gap: Actual flows <25% of annual requirement for a 1.5°C pathway.
________________________________________
Key Challenges
1. Scale of Investment: Renewable push, EV adoption, hydrogen, and carbon capture need trillions.
2. Private Sector Reluctance: High perceived risks, low returns on green infra.
3. International Commitments: $100 billion/year promised by developed nations — largely unmet.
4. State Finances: States lack fiscal space to support climate projects.
5. Institutional Capacity: Limited expertise in designing bankable green projects.
________________________________________
Government Initiatives
• National Green Hydrogen Mission (2023): ₹19,744 crore support.
• Energy Transition Fund: For phasing out coal plants.
• Climate Budget Tagging: Union and state budgets tagging climate-related expenditure.
• Public–Private Partnerships: EV infra, solar parks, offshore wind.
• G20 2023 Presidency: India pushed global consensus on scaling climate finance.
________________________________________
Global Dimension
• COP28 Outcomes: Loss and Damage Fund operationalized; India advocates equitable financing.
• Carbon Markets: India framing rules under Paris Agreement Article 6.
• Just Energy Transition Partnerships (JETPs): South Africa and Indonesia secured deals; India yet to finalize.

Significance
• Economic Growth: Green jobs, technology, and markets.
• Social Justice: Finance for adaptation critical for vulnerable communities.
• Diplomacy: India as leader of Global South climate justice narrative.
• Innovation: Finance enables R&D in storage, hydrogen, CCUS.

Way Forward
1. Domestic Resource Mobilisation: Expand green bonds, ESG-linked funds.
2. International Pressure: Push developed nations to honour $100 billion pledge.
3. Private Sector Role: De-risk projects via guarantees, blended finance.
4. Innovative Instruments: Carbon taxes, transition funds, climate insurance.
5. Capacity Building: Equip states/ULBs to design and execute climate projects.

Conclusion

Conclusion

Climate finance is the linchpin of India’s green transition. Without adequate resources, even the best policies cannot be implemented. By combining domestic innovation, international diplomacy, and private sector mobilisation, India can align growth with sustainability. For UPSC aspirants, this topic exemplifies how economics, environment, and global justice converge in policymaking.

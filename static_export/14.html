Judiciary Backlog — ADR and Mediation as Solutions

Economic Times | Date: 2024–25 • ~600 words

Introduction

India’s judiciary is burdened with an unprecedented backlog of over 5 crore cases across all levels. Despite digital reforms such as E-Courts, the pendency continues to grow. The Economic Times argued that Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) and mediation could offer scalable, cost-effective pathways to reduce pendency and improve access to justice. For UPSC aspirants, this issue illustrates the governance challenge of efficiency, inclusion, and institutional reform. ________________________________________ Scale of the Problem • Supreme Court: ~80,000 pending cases (2024). • High Courts: ~60 lakh cases pending. • District & Subordinate Courts: ~4.5 crore cases, constituting 90% of backlog. • Causes: o Shortage of judges and infrastructure. o Procedural delays and frequent adjournments. o Increased litigation from economic expansion and new rights-based laws. ________________________________________ What is ADR? • Definition: Mechanisms to resolve disputes outside traditional courts. • Forms: Arbitration, conciliation, mediation, Lok Adalats, and online dispute resolution. • Advantages: Faster, less adversarial, cost-efficient, preserves relationships. ________________________________________ Mediation in India 1. Institutional Push o Mediation Bill, 2023: Provided a legal framework for pre-litigation mediation, online mediation, and enforceability of mediated settlements. o Encouraged establishment of Mediation Councils and recognized mediation service providers. 2. Lok Adalats o Function under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987. o Have disposed of millions of cases, especially traffic challans and small disputes. 3. Commercial Disputes o Commercial Courts Act (2015) mandates pre-litigation mediation for certain disputes. o International Arbitration hubs (Mumbai, Delhi) emerging. ________________________________________ Challenges in ADR • Awareness: Many litigants still perceive courts as the only legitimate avenue. • Capacity: Shortage of trained mediators and arbitrators. • Enforcement: While Arbitration awards are enforceable, mediation historically lacked strong enforcement until the 2023 Act. • Resistance from Lawyers: Fear of reduced fees and influence in out-of-court settlements. ________________________________________ UPSC Relevance • GS-II (Polity): Judicial reforms, access to justice, ADR as good governance. • GS-III (Internal Security): Delays in justice contribute to social unrest. • GS-IV (Ethics): ADR reflects ethical principles of dialogue, empathy, and compromise. • Essay: o “Justice delayed is justice denied.” o “Dialogue and compromise as pillars of governance.” ________________________________________

Way Forward
1. Scale Up Mediation Infrastructure: Establish mediation centers at district level.
2. Capacity Building: Train mediators from law schools, retired judges, and community leaders.
3. Digital ADR: Expand Online Dispute Resolution (ODR) platforms, especially for e-commerce and financial disputes.
4. Public Awareness Campaigns: Inform citizens about benefits of mediation.
5. Judicial Integration: Encourage courts to refer suitable cases to ADR rather than adjudicate everything.

Conclusion

Conclusion

ADR and mediation cannot replace the judiciary but can complement it by offloading millions of disputes that do not require lengthy trials. By institutionalizing mediation and leveraging technology, India can build a faster, fairer justice system. For UPSC aspirants, this topic demonstrates the importance of reforms that combine efficiency with inclusivity in governance.

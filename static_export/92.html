India’s Skill Development Mission — Progress, Challenges, and Way Forward

The Hindu | Date: 2024 • ~600 words

Introduction

With over 65% of its population below 35 years, India’s demographic dividend depends on equipping youth with relevant skills. In 2024, The Hindu evaluated the performance of the Skill India Mission and its flagship initiatives like PMKVY, highlighting persistent challenges. For UPSC, this issue bridges economy, human capital, and governance. ________________________________________

Introduction

Current Status
• Skill India Mission (2015): Target of skilling 40 crore people by 2022 (extended).
• Enrolments: ~1.4 crore under PMKVY since inception.
• Placement Rate: ~45% post-training (varies across sectors).
• Sectoral Gaps: High demand in IT, logistics, healthcare; low uptake in agriculture.
• Global Context: India needs 29 crore skilled workers by 2030 (NSDC estimate).
________________________________________
Achievements
1. Awareness Generation: Skills mainstreamed in policy and public discourse.
2. Institutional Setup: NSDC, Sector Skill Councils operational.
3. Digital Push: Online training modules and skill apps introduced.
4. International Mobility: Skill agreements with Japan, UAE, Germany.
5. Women Participation: Rising enrolment in hospitality and healthcare.
________________________________________
Key Challenges
1. Low Placement Rates: Training not aligned with industry needs.
2. Quality Concerns: Many centres lack infrastructure and qualified trainers.
3. Informal Sector Neglect: Majority of workers outside skilling framework.
4. Mismatch in Aspirations: Youth prefer white-collar jobs over vocational roles.
5. Funding & Monitoring Issues: Leakages and uneven implementation.
________________________________________
Government Measures
• PMKVY (2015, revamped 2021): Focus on demand-driven training.
• National Apprenticeship Promotion Scheme: Incentivises industry to train.
• Skill Hubs & Kaushal Kendras: District-level centres.
• Samagra Shiksha: Vocational education integrated in schools.
• International Cooperation: Skill agreements for overseas placements.
________________________________________
Significance
• Economic Growth: Skilled workforce boosts productivity.
• Employment: Bridges gap between education and jobs.
• Social Equity: Skills empower poor, women, and marginalised.
• Global Competitiveness: Supports India’s ambition to be global talent hub.

UPSC Relevance
• GS-II (Governance): Welfare policies, human development.
• GS-III (Economy): Employment, demographic dividend.
• Essay:
o “Demographic dividend without skills is a demographic burden.”
o “Skilling is the currency of the 21st-century economy.”
• PSIR Paper II: Human capital and development policy.
________________________________________
Way Forward
1. Industry Alignment: Curriculum co-created with industry.
2. Focus on Informal Sector: Reskilling for construction, textiles, agriculture.
3. Digital Skilling: AI, robotics, data science training at scale.
4. Monitoring & Evaluation: Outcome-based funding.
5. Women & Rural Focus: Tailored skilling for local industries.
________________________________________
Conclusion

Conclusion

India’s skilling agenda is ambitious but uneven. A sharper focus on industry relevance, digital tools, and inclusivity will ensure that demographic dividend becomes an asset. For UPSC aspirants, this theme illustrates the link between governance, economy, and social justice.
